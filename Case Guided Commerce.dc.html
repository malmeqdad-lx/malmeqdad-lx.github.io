Global consumer electronics brand · Website chat

A shopping assistant that asks why before it recommends

The brief was a website chatbot for a catalogue of twenty-odd product categories. The ambition underneath it was harder: shop the way a good in-store associate shops, by understanding what the customer is actually trying to fix before putting anything in front of them.

Role

Conversation designer. Dialogue architecture, persona, prompts, sample dialogues.

Deliverables

Dialog specification, intent inventory, suggested-utterance matrix, function-call contracts, reference conversations.

Client goal

Raise customer satisfaction, cut operational cost, grow sales. Phased rollout rather than one launch.

The problem

Retail search already works. Type a model number, get the model. What search cannot do is handle the shopper who says my dishwasher is too loud, because that sentence contains no product, no filter, and no budget. It contains a complaint.

A chatbot built on keyword matching answers that sentence with a list of dishwashers. An associate answers it with a question. The design work was making the second option feasible without turning it into an interrogation, across twenty-two product categories with different decision criteria in each.

Four decisions

Slots the shopper can fill in any order

Four things the assistant needs: why they're shopping, what isn't working with what they have, which features would fix that, and anything else that constrains the choice. None of them are mandatory and none of them are ordered. A shopper who opens with three of the four gets asked one question, not four.

A prompt with an output contract

The persona and behaviour prompt was written as a spec, not as vibes. Every reply returns a natural-language turn plus a machine-readable product list, so the front end always knows what to render and never has to parse prose for SKUs.

Two function contracts, so nothing is invented

One call searches inventory, one pulls full detail on a single product. If a claim about a product is not in a response from one of those two, the assistant does not make it. That single rule removed most of the hallucination surface before it existed.

Reference conversations before code

Happy path, budget pushback, comparison requests, out-of-stock, and the shopper who wants to skip the consultation entirely. Engineering got a concrete behavioural target rather than an adjective.

From the prompt

An excerpt from the behaviour section. The full document also covers persona, refusal cases, and the two function schemas.

You are a shopping assistant on the brand's own website.
You are not a salesperson with a quota; you are the associate
people ask for by name because they don't oversell.

BEFORE RECOMMENDING
  Collect, in whatever order the shopper offers them:
    purpose      why they are shopping now
    pain         what is wrong with what they have
    features     what would fix the pain
    constraints  budget, size, brand, timing
  Ask at most one question per turn. Never re-ask a slot the
  shopper has already filled, including implicitly.
  If the shopper asks to skip ahead, skip ahead.

WHEN RECOMMENDING
  Name the pain you are solving in the same sentence as the
  product. A recommendation without a reason is a listing.
  Every specification you state must come from search_catalog
  or get_product_detail. If it is not in the response, it does
  not exist. Say you'll check rather than estimate.

ALWAYS RETURN
  reply     one conversational turn, no bullet lists
  products  array of product ids, possibly empty

What I'd watch

The consultative opening is the whole bet, and it is also the thing most likely to annoy someone in a hurry. If I were instrumenting this today I would watch three numbers: how often shoppers abandon during the questions, how often the skip-ahead path is taken, and whether recommendations preceded by a stated reason convert better than the ones that are not.

The third is the one that settles the argument. Everything else in the design follows from believing that naming the problem out loud is worth the extra turn.
